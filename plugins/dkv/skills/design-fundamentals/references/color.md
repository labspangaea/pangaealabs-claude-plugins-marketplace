# Colour

> Hue and its variations, schemes on the wheel, proportion, psychology, cultural meaning, colour
> modes, temperature, and the legibility floor.
>
> Unmarked statements are ordinary craft convention. **⚠ contested** marks something widely repeated
> but weakly supported — the note says what is actually known. Citations appear wherever a specific
> number or named study is doing the work.

## 1. Hue, tint, shade, tone

**Hue** is pure colour — red, blue, green — the thing the colour wheel is made of. Every other
colour in a palette is a hue that has been moved in one of three directions:

| Operation | Result | Feel |
|---|---|---|
| hue + **white** | **tint** | lighter, softer, more air |
| hue + **black** | **shade** | darker, heavier, more weight |
| hue + **grey** | **tone** | muted, dustier, less shouty |

This matters more than it looks. Most palettes that feel amateurish are made of pure hues at full
saturation, because pure hues all compete at the same volume. Tints, shades and tones are what give
you a *quiet* colour to put 60% of the canvas in — see §3.

## 2. Schemes on the wheel

Every scheme is a rule for picking hues by their position on the wheel:

| Scheme | Rule | Reads as |
|---|---|---|
| **Monochromatic** | one hue, then its tints + shades + tones | calm, cohesive, easy to get right, easy to make boring |
| **Complementary** | two hues opposite each other | maximum tension; the loudest pairing available |
| **Analogous** | 2–3 hues adjacent on the wheel | harmonious, natural, low-conflict |
| **Split-complementary** | one hue + the two neighbours of its opposite | complementary's contrast with less shouting |
| **Triadic** | three hues forming a triangle | vivid and balanced, hard to control |
| **Tetradic** | four hues forming a rectangle | rich; needs one clear dominant or it fragments |
| **Square** | four hues evenly spaced | as above, more evenly weighted |

The shape-on-the-wheel framing is the useful part: any scheme is just a polygon you rotate. Rotating
a triadic triangle gives you a different palette with identical structural balance.

Note that a scheme tells you *which hues*, never *how much of each*. That is §3's job, and skipping
it is why a technically-correct complementary palette can still look like a warning sign.

## 3. The 60/30/10 split

Allocate the canvas: **60% dominant, 30% secondary, 10% accent.** The dominant colour is usually the
quietest one (a tint or tone from §1), the accent is where the eye lands.

Two things the rule is easy to get wrong:

- **The meaningful contrast sits between the primary and the secondary**, not between the primary
  and the accent. If your 60 and your 30 are near-identical, the layout reads as flat no matter how
  loud the 10% accent is.
- **Derive the palette from the composition, not the other way round.** Decide what should dominate
  the frame, then assign the 60 to it. Picking three colours you like and then hunting for places to
  put them is how palettes end up arbitrary.

⚠ **contested** — 60/30/10 is an interior-design heuristic with no empirical basis; practitioners
describe it as an old rule of thumb rather than a finding. It remains genuinely useful as a *starting
proportion* that stops you from splitting a canvas evenly between three colours, which almost never
works. Treat it as a default to deviate from deliberately, not a law.

## 4. Colour psychology

Each colour carries associations that cut both ways. The negative column is the one designers skip,
and it is why "we'll use red, it's energetic" goes wrong on a medical dashboard.

| Colour | Positive | Negative | Typically used by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Red** | energy, urgency, appetite, passion | danger, aggression, alarm | food & beverage, clearance sales |
| **Orange** | optimism, warmth, enthusiasm | cheapness, caution | e-commerce, budget brands |
| **Yellow** | happiness, inspiration, attention | anxiety, cowardice, low legibility | children's products, warnings |
| **Green** | freshness, safety, growth, nature | inexperience, envy, sickliness | health, finance, environmental |
| **Blue** | stability, security, trust, calm | coldness, distance, corporate blandness | banking, insurance, enterprise software |
| **Purple** | luxury, mystery, creativity | pretension, artificiality | premium goods, beauty |
| **Pink** | softness, warmth, sweetness, care | frivolity, immaturity | beauty, confectionery, children's |
| **Brown** | natural, warm, grounded, artisanal | dullness, dirtiness | coffee, leather, organic goods |
| **Black** | serious, premium, authoritative | funereal, oppressive | luxury, fashion, high-end tech |
| **White** | pure, clean, simple, spacious | sterile, empty, clinical | healthcare, minimal brands |

⚠ **contested — "pink lowers heart rate."** This traces to Alexander Schauss (1979) and the
Baker-Miller pink prison-cell experiments. It did not replicate: a controlled study by Gilliam and
Unruh found no reduction in heart rate or grip strength, and a Santa Clara County jail study found
no difference in inmate aggression. Any observed effect appears to fade within ~15 minutes and may
reverse. **Keep pink → calm/soft as a learned association; drop the physiological claim.** The same
caution applies to "red raises heart rate and makes people hungry" — the association is real and
useful, the physiology is not established.

One qualifier is worth carrying throughout: colour psychology *isn't rigid* — what matters is the
feeling the specific combination builds. A practical test: **imagine your palette as an
interior** — a room painted in it. The vibe becomes obvious far faster than staring at swatches.

## 5. Meaning is learned, not innate

Colour meaning comes from culture, habit and marketing, not from the wavelength. This is the most
practically important section in this file, because it means **§4's table is audience-dependent** and
you must state which audience you are designing for.

- **Religion and nation** — green reads Islamic across much of the world; red+white reads
  nationalist in Indonesia; white is mourning in parts of East Asia and celebratory in the West.
- **Local convention** — in Indonesia a *bendera kuning* (yellow flag) marks a death in the
  neighbourhood; "toko oren"/"toko ijo" identify e-commerce brands by colour alone.
- **Marketing invented conventions we now read as natural.** The pink/blue gender split is the
  cleanest example, and it ran the *other way* first. In June 1918 the trade journal *Earnshaw's
  Infants' Department* stated the accepted rule was "pink for the boys, and blue for the girls" —
  reasoning that pink, "being a more decided and stronger color, is more suitable for the boy, while
  blue, which is more delicate and dainty, is prettier for the girl." The modern convention was not
  dominant until the **1950s** and not universal until a generation later (Jo B. Paoletti, *Pink and
  Blue*, 2012); a 1927 *Time* survey found major US department stores split six-to-four on which way
  round it went. There was never a clean flip — it was contested for decades, then settled by
  retail, as children's-product aisles were built out in blue and pink sections.

The takeaway: **colour is easier to recall than form**, so brands pick a colour first — but state
your assumed audience rather than asserting a universal meaning.

⚠ **contested — "colour increases brand recognition by 80%."** This figure is everywhere and is
miscited. It traces back to research on colour in *documents and graphs* (Hoadley, 1990) and to
colour-versus-black-and-white newspaper advertising — neither is about brand palettes. What is
supportable: colour aids recall and differentiation compared with monochrome. No specific
brand-recognition percentage is.

## 6. RGB vs CMYK

| | RGB | CMYK |
|---|---|---|
| Mixing | **additive** — light added, all three → white | **subtractive** — ink absorbs light, all → muddy brown, hence K |
| Channels | red, green, blue | cyan, magenta, yellow, **K = Key (black)** |
| For | screens, web, video, anything backlit | commercial print |
| Gamut | wider | narrower |

**K is Key, not "blacK"** — it comes from the key plate that carried detail in traditional printing.

The practical consequence is the gamut difference: RGB reproduces colours CMYK cannot, so converting
forces out-of-gamut colours to the nearest printable equivalent. Neon greens go muted, electric blues
soften, glowing reds go dusty — this is why print output looks duller than the screen version, and
it is not a mistake in your file. Design in the destination's mode from the start, soft-proof with
the actual press ICC profile if the job matters, and never pick a brand colour by how it looks on a
monitor if it will mostly live on paper.

## 7. Warm and cool

Reds, oranges, yellows and browns are **warm**; blues, greens and purples are **cool**. Warm colours
tend to feel closer, more energetic, more immediate; cool colours feel further away, calmer, more
composed. Use temperature as a fast way to separate figure from ground — a warm subject on a cool
field advances with almost no other help (see `gestalt.md` §4).

Temperature is relative, not absolute: a blue-leaning red is cool *next to* an orange-leaning red.
Palette clashes are usually two colours of the same nominal hue disagreeing about temperature.

## 8. The contrast floor — where theory meets shipping

> The step most colour advice skips, and the one that turns good palettes into unreadable ones.

Harmony rules say nothing about whether text can be read. That is a separate, measurable constraint,
and it is a hard floor rather than a preference.

**WCAG 2.2 minimums (Level AA):**

| Content | Minimum ratio |
|---|---|
| Body text | **4.5:1** |
| Large text (18pt, or 14pt bold) | **3:1** |
| UI components, graphical objects | **3:1** |
| Level AAA body / large | 7:1 / 4.5:1 |

**Colour must never be the only carrier of meaning** (WCAG SC 1.4.1). If red-versus-green is the
only thing distinguishing "failed" from "passed", the information is gone for a substantial share of
readers. Pair colour with a label, an icon, a shape, or position — always a second channel.

For palettes that must stay distinguishable under colour-vision deficiency, the **Okabe–Ito** eight
colour set is the standard reference — it deliberately avoids pure red and pure green:

```
#000000 black      #E69F00 orange       #56B4E9 sky blue    #009E73 bluish green
#F0E442 yellow     #0072B2 blue         #D55E00 vermillion  #CC79A7 reddish purple
```

**Blue + orange is the safest high-contrast pairing** — it survives every common form of colour
blindness, which is why so much data visualisation converges on it. Deuteranopia-type red-green
deficiency is the most common form, so a red/green pairing is the one to interrogate first.

Meeting the ratios is necessary, not sufficient: a design can pass every contrast check and still be
unusable if it encodes meaning in hue alone.
