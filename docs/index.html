PART 0

A colour, and the three things you can do to it

defines hue · needs nothing before it

Everything else on this page is built from this one idea, so it comes first.

A hue is a pure colour — red, blue, green — with nothing added. It is the raw material. Every other colour in a palette is a hue that has been moved in one of exactly three directions: add white and you get a tint, which feels lighter and airier. Add black and you get a shade, heavier and denser. Add grey and you get a tone, which is the same colour with the shouting taken out.

This matters more than it sounds. Palettes that feel amateurish are usually made of pure hues at full strength, all competing at the same volume. Tints, shades and tones are what give you a quiet colour — something you can put across most of a page without it fighting everything else.

PART 1

Two colours make a number, and the number has two floors

defines contrast ratio · built on hue

Put any two colours together and the difference between them can be measured. That measurement is a contrast ratio, written like 4.5:1. Identical colours are 1:1; black on white is the maximum, 21:1. It is arithmetic, not opinion — which makes it the one part of design review nobody can argue with.

There are two thresholds, and the second is the one almost everyone forgets. Body text needs 4.5:1 against its background. But non-text also needs 3:1 — the border of a button, a focus ring, an icon, the stroke of a chart. Anything a reader must see to understand what they are looking at.

The failure this catches is specific and common: a button whose label sits at a comfortable 13:1 while its border sits at 2:1. The text is perfectly readable. The thing telling you it is a button is not.

The plot above is this page measuring itself. The hairline used for decorative rules lands at 1.45:1 — far below both floors — which is exactly why it is never used to draw anything you need to see. Figure strokes use a different token at 4:1.

PART 2

Which colours, and how much of each

defines scheme · built on contrast ratio

Arrange every hue in a circle and the schemes become shapes you rotate. Complementary takes two hues opposite each other — the loudest pairing available. Analogous takes two or three neighbours, which is calm and hard to get wrong. Triadic takes a triangle. Rotate any of these shapes and you get a different palette with identical structure.

But a scheme only tells you which colours, never how much of each — and that second decision is where palettes usually fail. The common starting point is 60/30/10: sixty percent of the surface in a dominant colour, thirty in a secondary, ten in an accent. The part people get wrong is that the meaningful contrast belongs between the 60 and the 30. If those two are nearly identical, the layout reads flat no matter how loud the accent is.

⚠ 60/30/10 comes from interior design and has no empirical basis — practitioners describe it as an old rule of thumb. It is genuinely useful as a starting proportion, because it stops you splitting a canvas evenly three ways, which almost never works. It is a default to deviate from deliberately, not a law.

PART 3

Type sizes should come from a ratio, not from taste

defines modular scale · built on scheme

Pick a base size and one ratio, then multiply repeatedly. That is a modular scale, and it is the difference between a type system that looks deliberate and one that looks assembled. From a 16px base at a ratio of 1.25 you get 16 · 20 · 25 · 31 · 39 · 49. Every size is visibly related to every other because they share a common origin.

The ratio is a real choice. Small ratios like 1.125 give tight, dense steps suited to interfaces. Larger ones are dramatic and run out of usable sizes quickly.

⚠ The golden ratio, φ ≈ 1.618, is a perfectly good scale ratio — and it is not a law of beauty. That claim was popularised by Adolf Zeising in the 1850s; its empirical basis was Fechner's 1876 rectangle experiments, and the replications did not hold — Godkewitsch (1974) and Green's 1995 review found the preference disappears once stimulus range and context are controlled. What survives is a mild preference for a broad band of ratios roughly between 1.4 and 1.8, within which 1.618 is not special. Use it because it produces a strong dramatic step, not because it is magic. From 16px it gives 16 · 26 · 42 · 68 — four usable steps, which is why it suits a poster more than an interface.

PART 4

Line length is counted in characters

defines measure · built on modular scale

Measure is the length of a line of text, counted in characters rather than pixels — because what matters is how many words the eye crosses, not how wide the column is. The comfortable band for body text is 45 to 75 characters, with around 66 the usual optimum. On screen, 40 to 60 also works.

Both ends fail in their own way. Too short and the reading rhythm breaks every few words. Too long and the eye loses its place on the return sweep to the next line — you re-read a line, or skip one, and stop trusting the text.

Leading — the vertical space between lines — moves with it. Body text wants 120 to 150 percent of its size, and the longer the measure, the more leading it needs to keep the eye on the right line. Headlines go tighter, 1.0 to 1.2, because large type already has plenty of air.

PART 5

The eye groups things before it reads them

defines grouping cues · built on measure

Before anyone reads a word, their eye has already sorted the page into groups. Whatever your layout groups visually is the meaning the reader receives — regardless of what the content actually says. There are several cues that do this grouping, and crucially they are not equal in strength.

Weakest is similarity: things that look alike are read as related. Stronger is proximity: things placed near each other are read as related, and this beats appearance. Stronger still is common region: a shared box or background groups whatever is inside it, whatever the spacing. Strongest of all is uniform connectedness: a visible connector — a line, a rule, a bar — beats everything else, and it does not even need to touch the elements.

This ordering is the practically useful part. If two things must read as related but sit far apart, styling them alike is the weakest available fix — connect them or contain them instead. And when a grouping looks wrong although every element is styled correctly, look for an accidental box or line overriding your intent before touching the styling.

PART 6

Putting it together: what gets seen first

defines hierarchy · built on grouping cues

Hierarchy is the order in which a reader receives things. It is built from the previous parts: sizes from the scale, contrast from the ratio, grouping from the cues. Size and weight and contrast all push an element up or down the order, and used together they make the sequence unmistakable.

The commonest failure is promoting everything. When three elements are all the most important, none of them is. The working test: if you cannot name the single most important element in the composition, neither can the reader.

PART 7

Reading a design back

defines the review pass · built on hierarchy

With every term defined, the checks can run in order. The sequence is deliberate — the objective, pass/fail checks come first, so a beautiful but unreadable design is caught before anyone starts arguing about taste. Only then does judgement enter.

A critique that lists fifteen equal-weight findings does not get acted on. The output that matters is two or three highest-impact fixes, each naming the principle it comes from and the specific change to make.

What it refuses to claim

Four claims in wide circulation are marked contested rather than repeated, because design advice that states folklore confidently is the exact failure this method exists to avoid:

  • color.md 60/30/10 is an interior-design heuristic with no empirical basis
  • color.md pink lowers heart rate
  • color.md colour increases brand recognition by 80%
  • typography.md φ as a law of beauty

Where it stops

This method covers print and static graphic design — posters, packaging, labels, menus, signage, book covers, logos, slide decks, social posts. It does not cover websites, app or product interfaces, dashboards or components; those have their own constraints — interaction states, responsive behaviour, focus order — that a static review will not surface. It does not cover motion design, and it does not generate images. Knowing where a method stops is part of using it honestly.

The parts are numbered because they are a sequence — each is built from the term the one before it defined.
